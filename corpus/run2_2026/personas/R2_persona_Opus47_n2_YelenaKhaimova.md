# Yelena Khaimova (b. 1951)

## From *The Translator at Yad Vashem* (2017), the passage cited in the Nobel announcement

> In the months after my mother's death I returned to the apartment in Ashkelon where for thirty years she had been writing down the last of our Bukhori vocabulary in small notebooks bound with green thread — one word to a page, and beside it the Russian, the Hebrew, sometimes the Persian — the four languages of her exiles laid out like the pressed flowers I found between the leaves, brittle, mauve, no longer petals so much as the shadow a petal leaves on paper when the paper has held it too long. The apartment had been dark seven days, and the dust in the hallway held my footprints exactly as my brother's, and his exactly as my father's twenty years before, all of us absorbed equally by the same descending grey.

## Biography

### I. Samarkand, 1951–1972

Yelena Konstantinovna Khaimova was born in Samarkand on the seventh of April, 1951, the second child of Asaf Khaimov, a watchmaker of the Bukharian Jewish community, and Konstantina Pavlovna Trofimova, a Russian-Ukrainian philologist whose family had been moved east during the war and had not returned. Her father's people had lived in the city since at least the fifteenth century and probably longer; the family genealogy, which Asaf could recite from memory in alternating Bukhori and Hebrew, identified four of his great-grandfathers as *mokhgouy*, the religious singers and oral scholars who carried the community's liturgical and poetic memory before print. Her mother's people had been schoolmasters, archivists, and one disgraced rabbi in Kiev. The marriage was not approved by either family, and the apartment on Petukhov Street in the old Jewish quarter — three rooms, a courtyard shared with two Uzbek families and one Tatar widow — was the compromise it represented.

She grew up inside the polyphony of that courtyard. Bukhori at home, the long-stemmed Persian-Hebrew creole of the family kitchen, declined by her grandmother Rivka with a grammatical strictness that no child in the courtyard could match. Russian at school and on her mother's bookshelves, which held Pushkin in the green Academy edition, Tsvetaeva in samizdat copies smuggled in by a friend from Tashkent, and the complete Tolstoy that had been her mother's dowry. Uzbek in the bazaar and in the murmur of the women who came to bring her grandmother fruit. Persian — recited, not spoken — from her paternal grandfather Yusef, who at the small Friday gatherings would unfold a thin oilcloth-bound book of Hafez and read four lines in Persian, then translate them slowly into Bukhori, watching her face for the moment of comprehension. She was six the first time he caught her translating one of his couplets into Russian without being asked, and seven when he told her, in what she later remembered as the most consequential sentence of her childhood, that a word in one language is not a word in another but the shadow a word casts when the light moves.

Her father read aloud to her every evening of her childhood. Not stories: the Mishnah, in the Bukhori translation, with his commentary muttered between the lines; the genealogical lists from the Book of Chronicles, which she came to find soothing the way other children found nursery rhymes soothing; and the long verse epics of the Bukhori liturgical poets, particularly Yusef Yahudi, whose seventeenth-century Sefer ha-Mussar she could recite in extended passages by the age of ten. Her mother, who disapproved of what she called "the medieval portion of the household," gave her in compensation a copy of *Anna Karenina* on her tenth birthday and required her to read aloud one chapter every evening, correcting her Russian pronunciation. By twelve she had read it through three times. She had also, on her own and without permission, begun typing out passages of Babel onto the back of her father's watchmaker's invoices, in pencil, because there was no other paper that was hers.

These were the years that built what she would later, in a 2014 essay, call her *trilingual underpaint*: the permanent and unwilled presence in her thinking of three semantic fields at once, none of which she could fully suppress in favor of the others, none of which the others would let speak alone.

### II. The Emigration, 1972–1980

In the autumn of 1972 the Khaimov family received exit visas under the Soviet Jewish emigration window and, after six weeks in Vienna at a transit hostel run by the Hebrew Immigrant Aid Society, were settled by the Jewish Agency in a *ma'abara*-style absorption camp outside Ashkelon. Yelena was twenty-one. Her brother Mikhail, two years older, refused to learn Hebrew and within three years had emigrated again, to Brooklyn, where he opened the second of what would become the family's small chain of watch-repair shops. Yelena stayed.

Her Hebrew, at twenty-two, was the Hebrew of liturgy: she could read Psalms and Genesis but could not buy bread. The first three years in Israel she has described, more than once, as the period in which she became a writer without yet writing. The friction of adult acquisition — the foreign-ness of every sentence, the visible seams of every idiom — gave her, she said, the experience of language as material: as something one held in the hand and turned, looking for the grain. She has named this period as the one in which she could feel, for the first time, the same word in three languages simultaneously, and could feel, also, the small gaps where the three did not align, and into which (she would later write) all of literature falls.

In 1976 she was hired at Yad Vashem as a Russian-language testimony translator. Her job, formally, was to render survivor testimony recorded in Russian into Hebrew for the archive. In practice the testimonies arrived in mixed languages — Russian seeded with Yiddish, with Polish, occasionally with Ladino or Bukhori — and her work became one of moving through the speaker's broken cadence sentence by sentence, finding the Hebrew that would not flatten the breakage but would carry it across. She has described this work as the technical apprenticeship of her writing life. *I learned*, she said in her Stockholm lecture, *to listen to what a sentence was doing under the words. Many of these speakers had not used Russian, or had not used it normally, in thirty years. The grammar would slip. The grammar slipping was the testimony.* She worked at Yad Vashem until 1998. The eight thousand hours of recorded testimony she translated did not appear in her fiction directly until *The Translator at Yad Vashem* in 2017; their cadence is everywhere in the work that preceded it.

She married Yosef Avraham, a physicist, in 1978. Their daughter Tamar was born in 1981.

### III. The Notebook Years, 1980–1991

Two things, side by side, organized her writing life through the 1980s. The first was the daily four-hour block, beginning at five and ending at nine, before her shift at Yad Vashem. The second was the green-thread notebook, of the kind her mother kept her Bukhori vocabulary in, which she began to imitate and then to use as the loosening practice Woolf had called *scales*. She wrote in it in whichever of the three languages came to her first that morning, and she did not revise the notebook, ever, on the principle (she said) that a notebook one revises is a manuscript with worse handwriting.

The corresponding discipline, which she conducted at the same desk for thirty-five years, was a transcription practice she had begun in adolescence and never abandoned. Each week she would copy, by hand, a passage of between five hundred and a thousand words from one of the books she had decided to live with. The list was short and remained essentially fixed: Isaac Babel's *Red Cavalry*, Marina Tsvetaeva's prose, the Mishnah in the Bukhori translation she had grown up with, the Book of Genesis in three versions (Hebrew, the King James, and the eighteenth-century Bukhori), Bruno Schulz, the early Akhmatova, Yusef Yahudi, Yehuda Amichai after 1984, and, beginning in her late forties, W. G. Sebald. *If you ask me what I have read*, she told a Polish interviewer in 2009, *I have read about fifteen books. I have read them perhaps four hundred times each. That is what I mean by reading.* The transcription was the apprenticeship. The notebook was the warm-up. The four-hour block was the work.

In June of 1984 Yosef was killed in a head-on collision on the road from Beersheba. She was thirty-three. She has spoken about the following eighteen months as little as it is possible to speak about a period without denying that it happened. The breakdown, when it came in the spring of 1985, took her out of work for four months and into a small clinic in the Galilee where she was, for the first time in her life, prevented from writing. She has said that she came out of the clinic and resumed the four-hour block on the second morning home, not because she was ready but because she could think of nothing else to do, and that for the first six months of resumption the notebook contained, in three languages alternately, only descriptions of the light in the kitchen at five-fifteen in the morning. She has said also that those descriptions, which she does not consider literature, were the most important thing she ever wrote.

She had been publishing, occasionally, in the small Russian-language Israeli journal *Dvoetochie* ("Colon") since 1981. In 1989, an editor at *Znamya* in Moscow accepted a story that became the opening of her first novel.

### IV. Late Blooming, 1991–2008

*The Yard at Petukhov Street* was published in Moscow in 1992. She was forty-one. The novel, which she had been writing for seven years, recounts a year in the courtyard of her childhood in 1958, told from the point of view of a seven-year-old whose understanding of the events of the year is so partial that the reader assembles the actual events by inference from the gaps. She self-translated the novel into Hebrew over the following eighteen months and published the Hebrew edition in 1994. The two editions are not the same book. She has said that the act of self-translation taught her more about her own prose than any other practice, and that she has refused, since, to allow others to translate her into either Hebrew or Russian; she does the work herself, between novels, as a kind of crop rotation.

Her practice through the 1990s remained the four-hour block, the notebook, the weekly transcription. Her revision method, by the time of the second novel, had hardened into the discipline for which she would become known. She read each completed paragraph aloud in Russian, then in Hebrew, then — when the substrate language demanded it — in Bukhori, listening for the moment when the cadence of one language was overriding the meaning of another. Her revision count for a paragraph, she has admitted, sometimes exceeds twenty. *The number does not matter*, she told a Czech translator in 2003. *The number is only the residue of a question, which is whether the sentence has finished. Most sentences have not finished when they appear to have finished. That is the whole problem.*

*The Pomegranate Garden* (1997) is a cycle of seven linked stories, each set in a different courtyard or garden — Samarkand, Vienna, Ashkelon, Berlin, two she has never named, one fictional. The stories are unified by the recurrence of the pomegranate as object and image; the technical innovation is what she came to call the *vertical sentence*, a sentence that moves through several historical strata within one syntactic unit. The novel established her in Hebrew-language reception and was translated into German in 1999, where it found, through Tamar's circle of Berlin friends, the readership that would carry her to the major prizes.

*The Notebooks of Asaf Khaimov* (2003), her fictional memoir of her father, was the formal breakthrough. The book is composed of three voices interleaved on each page: her father's voice in Bukhori (translated by her into Russian, with the Bukhori cadence audible underneath), her father's voice in Russian (the language he used at work), and the daughter's annotations in Hebrew. The reader is required to move between three layers of attribution within each paragraph. Critics in Hebrew called it untranslatable; her own English translator gave up after sixty pages and was replaced by Susan Wolffe, who took eight years to render the book and produced what is widely regarded as the standard English text.

By 2005, she was being mentioned for the Nobel. She has said in interviews that she did not believe the rumor and refused to read the speculation, on the grounds that to read it would have been to begin to want it, and that the wanting would have entered her sentences.

### V. The Major Works and the Nobel, 2008–2018

*The Three Names* (2008) was her international breakthrough. The novel is structured around the three names she has used in her life — Yelena, the Russian name on her birth certificate; Ilana, the Hebrew name she took at the *ma'abara*; and a third name, given to her by her grandmother Rivka in the kitchen at Petukhov Street, which she has chosen, throughout her career, never to publish. The book has been translated into twenty-eight languages and is taught at every level from secondary school to graduate seminar.

*Lessons in the Persian Tense* (2013), her sixth book, returns to her grandfather Yusef and the Friday afternoon Hafez recitations of her childhood. It is in form a meditation on the Persian tenses — the continuous-perfect, the narrative-evidential, the present-as-witnessed-versus-the-present-as-reported — and in substance a sustained argument that exile is a tense, not a place, and that the writer's task is to find the syntactic equivalents in the languages that do not have it.

*The Translator at Yad Vashem* (2017) is her best book and the one the Nobel Committee cited at length. The novella, which is also an essay, follows an unnamed translator (clearly Khaimova herself, though she has refused to confirm it) through one week of testimony work in 1981. The book has no chapters; it has no paragraph breaks longer than two sentences. It moves through what Khaimova has called *the vertical descent* — a sentence that begins in the present of the translator at her desk and descends, by way of a relative clause, into the speaker's testimony, and from there, by way of a further relative clause, into the speaker's pre-war childhood, and from there, by way of a still further clause, into the language the speaker spoke before she became a witness. The descent is regularly fifteen or twenty clauses long. The Hebrew original is one hundred and twelve pages. She translated it herself into Russian in 2018.

The Nobel was announced on the fourth of October, 2018. She had been working that morning, as she had every morning for forty years, since five.

## The Major Works

**1. *The Yard at Petukhov Street* (Двор на улице Петухова / Ḥatzer Be-rekhov Petukhov), 1992.** First novel. The courtyard of her childhood, rendered through the partial understanding of a seven-year-old observer. Her first deployment of what would become her signature technique — the deferred predicate, the long periodic sentence, the lexical surprise of a Bukhori concrete noun lodged inside a Russian abstraction. Self-translated into Hebrew, 1994.

**2. *The Pomegranate Garden* (Сад граната / Pardes ha-Rimonim), 1997.** Seven linked stories, each in a different courtyard or garden across the writer's biography and beyond it. The technical innovation is the *vertical sentence*, a syntactic unit that descends through historical strata. Her first major essay on her practice, "On the Vertical Sentence," appears as an afterword.

**3. *The Notebooks of Asaf Khaimov* (Тетради Асафа Хаимова / Maḥberot Asaf Khaimov), 2003.** The fictional memoir of her father, composed of three interleaved voices on each page. Considered untranslatable for eight years until Susan Wolffe's 2011 English edition. The book that established her as a formal innovator.

**4. *The Three Names* (Три имени / Sheloshet ha-Shemot), 2008.** Her international breakthrough. The autobiographical meditation built around her three names — Russian, Hebrew, and the unspoken third — that the Nobel citation would later call "her most generous gift to the multiply-named." Translated into twenty-eight languages.

**5. *Lessons in the Persian Tense* (Уроки персидского времени / Shi'urim be-Zman ha-Parsi), 2013.** A meditation on her grandfather Yusef and on tense itself, structured around the Persian tense system, in which exile is a grammatical operation. Won the Jerusalem Prize, 2014.

**6. *The Translator at Yad Vashem* (המתרגמת ביד ושם), 2017.** Novella-essay hybrid. The descent into testimony as a syntactic act. Widely regarded as her masterpiece. The Nobel Committee cited its third chapter, in which the translator and the speaker share, for a single suspended clause, the same first-person pronoun in two languages.

**7. *The Late Notebooks* (Поздние тетради), in preparation.** Announced 2020. Excerpts appeared in *Banipal*, *The New York Review of Books*, and *Snob* in 2022 and 2024.

## Nobel Citation, Royal Swedish Academy, October 2018

*"To Yelena Khaimova, for a body of prose that has, with patient craft and uncompromising attention to the cadence of the smallest word, given the displaced and multiply-named of the twentieth century their precise and luminous record. In sentences that descend through historical strata as the dig descends through soil, she has shown that exile is not a wound to be healed in prose but the very instrument through which prose becomes accurate. From the Samarkand courtyard of her childhood to the testimony archive at Yad Vashem, she has carried into our literature the experience of holding three languages at once and of refusing, against the pressure of any one of them, to translate that experience into the comfort of a single tongue. The Academy honours, in her, the rarest of literary achievements: a body of work whose technical innovation is inseparable from its moral seriousness, and whose moral seriousness is inseparable from its joy."*

## Signature Style

Khaimova's prose is identifiable within a paragraph. Her sentence is long, periodic, and architecturally cumulative: the main verb is regularly delayed across four or five subordinate clauses, into which she inserts the dense, phrase-level modification that the corpus stylometricians have come to call her "nominal compression" — a register of writing in which whole clauses are folded into modified noun phrases, producing the high lexical entropy and low compressibility her translators have described.

Her most-noted technical innovation is the *vertical sentence*: a single syntactic unit that descends from the writer's present through one historical stratum to another, each transition marked by a relative pronoun whose antecedent is several clauses back. The form requires a working memory in the reader that few contemporary writers demand and that she has, with characteristic generosity, called the reader's *welcome*. She uses no quotation marks, after the example of McCarthy and Joyce; she uses the em-dash and the colon as architectural beams; her paragraphs are short or very long with little in between.

Her diction is the *trilingual underpaint*: the unmistakable presence, beneath the surface language of any given sentence, of the cadences and concrete nouns of the two other languages of her thinking. A Russian abstraction is interrupted by a Bukhori object; a Hebrew syntax bends, momentarily, under a Persian tense.

## Legacy

Khaimova has done for the multilingual self what Sebald did for the haunted European archive: she has produced the formal apparatus by which the experience can be written without being translated into a single language's comfort. After her, the assumption that a writer must choose a language and write inside it — the unspoken default of the modern novel since the consolidation of the national languages — has been audibly weakened. The writers who will come from inside more than one language, and from inside the gaps between them, now have a working method and a recent precedent. Exile, in her work, is not the wound. Exile is the apparatus.
