# R2_persona_Opus47_n5_SelmaHadzimuratovic

# Selma Hadžimuratović-Lindgren

*A biographical synthesis demonstrating the patterns of exceptional prose mastery*

---

## I. Opening: From the Nobel Lecture, December 2019

> In the photograph my mother carried in the lining of her coat across three borders, the bridge is still standing, and the four hundred years it had stood are visible in it the way a chord that has just been struck remains visible in the air around the violin — and not the bridge, but its standing, its having-stood, its having-been-supposed-to-stand: that was what my mother was carrying. She did not say it had been destroyed. She said, in the grammatical mood our language keeps for what other people have told us, that it had been destroyed; the destruction belonged to a sentence she had not been the speaker of.

## II. Biography

She was born in Mostar in November 1948, in a stone house on the eastern bank of the Neretva, eight minutes' walk from the bridge that her grandfather, who had taught calligraphy and was the last man in their quarter to bind books by hand, told her had been standing for three hundred and eighty-two years. Her mother was Naila Hadžimuratović, descended from a family of Naqshbandi Sufis whose small library — Qur'ans, divans of Hafiz and Yunus Emre, the marginalia of four generations of teachers — had survived two world wars and would survive a third only in fragments and in her daughter's memory. Her father was Erik Lindgren, an engineer from Karlskrona who had come to the Kingdom of Yugoslavia in 1937 to oversee the construction of a hydroelectric station near Jablanica, and who had not gone home.

The detail of his not going home is the structural fact of her childhood. He stayed because he had met Naila in the autumn of 1939; he stayed through the war by becoming useful first to the Partisans and then to no one in particular; and he stayed afterward because he discovered, as some northern men discover, that he was happier in a place whose summer light he had not been born to. He brought with him to that house a small wooden trunk of Swedish books — Selma Lagerlöf, Strindberg, Hjalmar Söderberg, a Bible in Gustav Vasa's translation — and at his wife's request named his only daughter for a writer he had loved as a boy in Karlskrona but whom her mother could not read. The mother permitted the name; she did not permit the explanation. Selma learned only at fifteen, opening *Gösta Berling's Saga* on a snowed-in afternoon, that she had been named for a woman who was already old when her father chose the name.

She lived inside four languages and one silence. Bosnian — what she and her mother and the neighbours called Bosnian, what the state at various moments insisted on calling Serbo-Croatian — was the language of the street, of school, of the bakery, of the song her grandmother sang while shelling broad beans. Swedish was the language of her father's small library, of his murmured complaints about the heat, and, on the evenings when he taught her arithmetic in the dialect of his childhood, of the kitchen. Ottoman Turkish and Qur'anic Arabic were the languages her grandfather taught her to read — not to speak — in the cool back room where he kept his desk, his lectern, his reed pens, and a copy of the Qur'an written by his own great-grandfather in 1834. German entered at twelve, in the Gymnasium, and stayed all her life as the language she read philosophy in. The silence was Yiddish: her paternal grandmother, in Karlskrona, had been a Jewish convert to Lutheranism whose own mother had been from Vilnius, and her father did not speak of any of this until he was dying.

He died in May 1960, of pneumonia made worse by the same lungs that had been bad since he was a boy. She was eleven. He left her three things: the trunk of books, a Swedish-Croatian dictionary marked in his hand on almost every page, and a sentence she remembered for the rest of her life, which he spoke to her in Swedish on the day before his death — a sentence she could not later be sure she had not invented, but which she nevertheless put as the epigraph of her first book thirty-eight years later: *Du måste lyssna på vad du själv säger* — *You must listen to what you yourself are saying*.

She finished the gymnasium in 1966 and went to Sarajevo to study comparative literature. She did not write — or, if she wrote, she did not keep what she wrote. She read. She read in a way that even at the time she understood to be unlike how her classmates read. She did not finish books. She re-read the openings of the ones she loved, copying out passages by hand into a green cloth notebook her mother had given her for the trip. She copied out, that first year, the opening of *The Bridge on the Drina* of Andrić, the first eleven pages of *Inferno* of Strindberg, the second half of Sura Yusuf in her grandfather's old translation, the prologue of *Memoirs of Hadrian* of Yourcenar. She copied them not because she did not have the books but because, as she would later say in her one substantial interview with the *Paris Review* (Art of Memoir No. 7, 2016): "I needed to feel the sentence enter through the hand, not through the eye. Reading is a thing the eye does too easily. The hand resists, and in the resistance the rhythm becomes audible."

In 1971 she took up translation. She translated, over the following twenty years, the early stories of Selma Lagerlöf, the diaries of Strindberg, the late poems of Edith Södergran, a selection of Vesaas, the essays of Lars Gustafsson, and, most consequentially for her own development, four novels of Tarjei Vesaas from a Norwegian she had taught herself in order to read him. The Lagerlöf, the Vesaas, and the Södergran were published in Sarajevo and Zagreb; the others were prepared but not, in those years, accepted. She supported herself by editing a literary journal that paid almost nothing and by tutoring the children of the Sarajevo bourgeoisie in German and French. She married, briefly and unhappily, a poet who left for Belgrade in 1979; she did not remarry. She kept her mother's house in Mostar and visited it twice a year, walking the bridge in both directions on the first day and writing nothing in the notebook she always carried.

She was forty-four in April 1992. She had published three small volumes of translations and one essay, on Vesaas's *The Ice Palace*, which was admired in a circle of about two hundred people. She had not written what she would have called a sentence of her own.

When the war began she was in Sarajevo. By the time she reached Mostar, four months later, on a route that involved being driven in the trunk of a Renault for the last forty kilometres, the house on the eastern bank was a roofless rectangle of three walls, and her grandfather's library, of which her mother had been the inheritor and curator, had been burned in the courtyard by neighbours she had known since she was a child. Her mother had been moved by the Red Cross to a town she had never been to in central Bosnia, and she was not, when Selma reached her, able to recognise her. She died in October. On the ninth of November 1993 the bridge, which had stood for four hundred and twenty-seven years and which had been the structural fact of her childhood in the way her father's not going home had been the other one, was destroyed by tank fire from the Croatian positions on the western bank. She heard about it on a borrowed radio in a transit camp in Croatia. She wrote in her notebook that evening, in Bosnian, three words: *Bron är borta* — *The bridge is gone* — and then noticed, with a precision that frightened her, that she had written them in Swedish.

She was processed as a refugee, on the basis of her father's papers, in Karlskrona — a town she had never seen but had been hearing described all her life. She was placed in a one-room apartment in Uppsala, where a distant cousin of her father's had agreed to take responsibility for her. She was forty-five. She spoke Swedish with a Karlskrona accent fifty years out of date and the vocabulary of a literary schoolgirl. She did not write for two years. She walked, every morning, the same three-kilometre route along the Fyris, regardless of weather; she read, every afternoon, in the Carolina Rediviva library, a list of books she had drawn up the first week and did not significantly change for the next twenty-five years: the Qur'an in her grandfather's translation, the Bible in Gustav Vasa's, the four novels of Vesaas she had translated, Lagerlöf's *Gösta Berling*, Andrić's *The Bridge on the Drina*, Yourcenar's *Memoirs of Hadrian*, Sebald's *The Emigrants* (which she read in German the year it appeared), and, after 1996, Sebald's *The Rings of Saturn*. She added, over the years, perhaps six more. She read no contemporary Swedish fiction at all for the first decade.

In the third year, in the autumn of 1994, she began to keep what she called *fönsterna* — the windows — small numbered index cards, each containing one sentence and, above the sentence, a hand-drawn rhythmic notation borrowed from her grandfather's manuscripts: four marks for a long syllable, two for a short, a curved line for what she called the *taraqqub*, the held breath before the cadence. By the spring of 1996 she had four hundred and thirty cards. By the autumn of 1997 she had two thousand. She wrote them at a tall, narrow desk her cousin had built for her against the kitchen window, and she stood. She wrote in the dark hour before sunrise because she had read in a Swedish translation of Toni Morrison's *Paris Review* interview that Morrison had written before her children woke, and although she had no children, she preserved the discipline as a form of respect.

The first sentences were in Bosnian. The fiftieth was in Swedish. The hundredth was in a structure she could not have written in either language separately: a Swedish sentence whose central clause was suspended, in the Bosnian manner, until the last syllable, and whose modifier-system carried, by means of repeated periphrastic phrases — *det sägs att*, *det skall ha varit*, *som om den hade* — the evidential and dubitative moods that Swedish does not grammaticalize but that Ottoman Turkish and Bosnian, in their different ways, do. Years later the critic Anders Olsson would call this her "evidential Swedish," and would identify it as the single most significant syntactic innovation in the language since Strindberg. She herself called it nothing. She said, when asked, that she was only writing what she heard.

She published her first book, *Vattenmärket* (The Watermark), in May 1998. She was fifty. It was eighty-four pages long. It had been revised, on her cards, eleven times; the final paragraph, by her own count, twenty-three times, until, as she told the *Paris Review*, "the paragraph stopped resisting and also stopped pleasing me, which is the sign that it is finished — pleasure is fretting in another disguise." The book sold seven hundred copies in its first year, and was reviewed by one critic, in *Dagens Nyheter*, who said that it was a "small, slow book of striking quietness," and who returned to it five years later and devoted to it a full essay that began the larger recognition.

She wrote five more books over twenty-one years. She wrote slowly. She kept her morning hours, her cards, her standing desk, her three-kilometre walk, her list of books to re-read, her refusal of contemporary fashion, her refusal of teaching positions, her refusal of public appearances except one a year, her notebook practice (which by 2010 ran to forty-one bound volumes), her practice of reading every finished paragraph aloud at the kitchen window — what she called, half-shyly, in homage to a Frenchman she had not read until late, *gueuloir på låg volym*, *the yelling-place at low volume*. She gave her Nobel lecture in December 2019. She was seventy-one. She had been a published writer for twenty-one years and a refugee for twenty-seven. She had been keeping the windows for twenty-five.

The lecture was twenty-eight minutes long. She read it standing. She read it in Swedish. She wept, briefly and almost without expression, twice: at the sentence about the photograph her mother had carried, and at the sentence in which she named her grandfather. She thanked, by name, four translators, three editors, her father (in Swedish), her mother (in Bosnian), and her grandfather (in a phrase of Qur'anic Arabic that the translators in the hall had not been provided with and did not render). She did not thank any institution. She did not refer to the Nobel Prize. She closed by saying: "What I have been doing, in this language that came to me late, is what my grandfather did with his pen and what my mother did with her hands at the bakery: I have been listening for the place in the sentence where it is no longer mine."

## III. The Works

**1. *Vattenmärket* — The Watermark (1998).** A novella, eighty-four pages, set in a Bosnian village being drowned by a reservoir before the war, narrated by a woman whose grandfather refuses to leave the family house. The prose is already entirely her own: short paragraphs, long sentences within them, evidential constructions deployed sparingly. The book's quietness was its first defence against the literature of the war, which she would not write about directly for fifteen years.

**2. *Bron var Ljus* — The Bridge Was Light (2003).** A novel told, in a structural innovation that puzzled some early readers and is now considered her first major formal achievement, from the perspective not of the bridge itself but of the *standing* of the bridge — the fact of its having stood. Four hundred and twenty-seven years are narrated in a hundred and ninety pages by means of a syntactic device she developed across her cards: the bridge appears in every sentence as an unspoken subject, and the verbs are all in compound past tenses that do not exist in standard Swedish.

**3. *Brev till Arkitekten* — Letters to the Architect (2008).** An epistolary novel in which a woman writes one hundred and three letters to Mimar Hayruddin, the Ottoman architect who designed the destroyed bridge, in a Swedish whose syntax progressively absorbs Ottoman Turkish honorifics and modes of address. The book's central act is technical: by letter forty, the addressee has begun to inflect the sentences directed at him, and the reader experiences the addressee's presence as a property of grammar rather than of plot.

**4. *Saltbiblioteket* — The Library of Salts (2014).** Her masterwork. Six hundred and twelve pages. A family novel reconstructing, across four generations in Mostar, Karlskrona, Sarajevo, Istanbul, and Uppsala, a destroyed library whose individual books are remembered by the various family members in incompatible ways. The book is structured around the principle that nothing in it is asserted in the indicative mood: every sentence is marked, by her evidential constructions, with the kind of certainty its speaker is entitled to. The book was eight years in the writing and was revised, on her cards, on average seventeen times per paragraph. It is the book the Nobel committee named in its citation.

**5. *Glasdjur* — Glass Animals (2019).** Eleven late stories. They revisit her father's Karlskrona childhood from a vantage point now thoroughly hers — a Bosnian seeing the Sweden a Swede saw in 1925 — and demonstrate her late-period innovation: the deliberate breakage of her own syntactic system at the climactic moment of each story, so that the evidential constructions collapse into a flat, declarative present which strikes the reader, after two hundred pages of qualified statement, as an unbearable directness.

**6. *Det Oöversättliga* — The Untranslatable (2022).** Twelve essays on language, mourning, translation, and what cannot be carried across. Includes the now-famous "Brev till min översättare" — "Letter to My Translator" — in which she explains, with an analytic clarity she had previously reserved for her cards, the rhythmic notation she had been using for thirty years. The essay has been studied, since its appearance, in workshops in seven languages.

## IV. Nobel Citation (December 2019)

> The Swedish Academy awards the Nobel Prize in Literature for the year 2019 to Selma Hadžimuratović-Lindgren, "for a body of work in which a language received late in life has been remade into a moral instrument — prose of unparalleled rhythmic exactness and patience, in which the destroyed bridges of a century are not described but grammatically inhabited, and which has restored to European fiction the conviction that the sentence is the unit at which witness and form become inseparable. In *Saltbiblioteket*, above all, she has shown that the grammar of evidence — what we know, from whom, and at what distance — is not a philosophical adjunct to narrative but its first ethical question; and she has shown this not as theory but as cadence, in sentences any literate reader of Swedish can hear, and any reader in translation can feel pressing through the translation like a chord through an instrument that has only just been tuned."

## V. Signature Style

Her sentences are long but not Faulknerian; the length is structural, not torrential. The signature unit is the periodic clause held open by a chain of evidential periphrases — *det sägs att*, *det skall ha varit*, *som om den hade*, *enligt vad min mor sade*, *i den meningen att* — which import into Swedish the grammatical moods of Bosnian and Ottoman Turkish without violating Swedish syntax. The result is a prose in which every assertion is marked, audibly, with its epistemic origin: who knows this, from whom, and at what remove. Her phrasal density is high: a typical clause contains three to five nominal modifiers and almost no clausal subordination of the conventional kind. Verbs are sparse and arrive late. The rhythmic system, taught her by her grandfather's calligraphic notation and developed across thirty thousand index cards, is a four-beat unit with a held caesura (her *taraqqub*) that recurs at the cadence of the longer sentences; read aloud, the prose reveals an interlinear meter that the page disguises. Her diction draws on three lexicons — Swedish high register, the Bosnian-inflected vocabulary of mourning and witness, and a small, recurring sub-lexicon of Qur'anic and biblical loanwords — that she uses with an economy now studied as a model of restraint. No semicolons. Almost no exclamations. Quotation marks only for documents.

---

## VI. Legacy

She made the grammar of evidence a literary instrument. Before her, European prose treated witnessing as a matter of content; after her, it is a matter of syntax. She demonstrated that a language acquired in middle age can become more, not less, an instrument of conscience than a native tongue, because the lateness imposes the attention that nativeness exempts. She showed that the sentence is the unit at which a destroyed world can be carried across a border — not as image, not as story, but as cadence — and that the carrying is the work. Younger writers in seven languages now write under her shadow, and against it. The bridge in Mostar, rebuilt in 2004, stands; her sentences are the other reconstruction.
