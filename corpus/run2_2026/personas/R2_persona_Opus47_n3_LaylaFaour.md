# R2_persona_Opus47_n3_LaylaFaour

*Nobel Prize in Literature, 2019*

---

## Opening: From the Nobel-Winning Novel *A Casa da Tia Sombra* (The House of Aunt Shadow, 2018)

> My father set type in three alphabets. In the back room of the print-shop on Rue Sursock, where the kerosene lamp threw onto the wall a shadow vaster than any of us, he taught me that every language is a private weather: Arabic the sirocco that arrives already inside the house, French the harmattan one is given at school and learns to wear, the Portuguese my mother had carried across the sea a damp particular sweetness that smelled, in my child's nose, of cardamom and ironed cotton.

## Biography

She was born Layla Maria Faour-Andrade in Beirut on the fourteenth of October, 1957, the only child of Boutros Faour, a Maronite Christian typesetter from a village above Bcharré, and Maria Eugênia Andrade, a Brazilian doctoral researcher in Semitic philology who had arrived in Lebanon in 1953 to study the Syriac liturgy and never quite arrived home. The household into which Layla was born was, by her own later account, "a room with three doors and four winds." Her father spoke Arabic to the bread, French to the bank, Italian to the radio, and the rough Aramaic of his grandfather's church to his god. Her mother spoke Portuguese to her daughter and to the houseplants, and a kind of slow studious Arabic to everyone else, the Arabic of a woman whose vocabulary was larger than her grammar. There was no language in which a single fact stayed flat. To ask for milk was to choose, each morning, what kind of person to be.

This trilingual condition is the most easily named of Layla Faour's inheritances, and in nearly every profile written about her since the Nobel it is named first, often as if it explained everything. It does not explain everything, but it explains the central architectural fact of her prose, which is that no English sentence she ever wrote — and she wrote none; she has been translated into thirty-seven languages — sits at rest on its surface. Beneath every Portuguese noun in her novels there is the rumor of an Arabic root, beneath every cadence the metric memory of the Maronite chant her father hummed while he set type, beneath every conjunction a French formality her teachers at the Lycée Abdel-Kader pressed into her between 1963 and 1975. She has said in interviews — when she gives them, which is once every several years — that she does not so much *write in Portuguese* as *write toward Portuguese from a great distance, like someone calling across a valley*.

The second inheritance was synesthesia, of a sort her mother had it too and her mother's father before her. Particular letters carried, for Layla, particular temperatures: the Arabic *ع* (ayn) was the warm dust under a stone lifted in summer; the Portuguese *ç* was the cold of a tile floor in winter; the French *é* was an iron just hot enough to scorch linen. She would say, much later, in the 2017 Paris Review interview with Idra Novey, that she had not understood until adolescence that other people did not have these sensations and that the word *cardamomo* did not, for them, smell of itself on the page. The condition was useful in a way she only learned to use slowly: it meant that the wrong word, the merely correct word, the word that meant the right thing but rang the wrong temperature, was physically uncomfortable to leave on a page. Her later revision practice — about which more below — has the obsessive, almost dermatological quality of someone removing splinters.

The third inheritance was her father's print-shop. Boutros Faour was a typesetter of the old kind, a man who set Arabic right-to-left and French left-to-right in the same forme without losing his place, and who could be heard, behind the press, reciting under his breath the lines he was setting. Layla spent every afternoon between the ages of six and seventeen in the back room of that shop on Rue Sursock, doing her homework at a small table beneath a window that gave onto a courtyard with a lemon tree, reading whatever her father was setting that week: catechisms, French agricultural manuals, the newsletter of the Beirut Greek Orthodox Women's Association, a slim and quarrelsome poetry quarterly called *Ash-Shi'r* whose editors would sometimes appear in person to argue with her father about the placement of a single colophon. She has said that she learned to read the way other children learn to swim, by being put into the water without ceremony. By eight she had read the Arabic Bible of the Vatican Press end to end three times because it happened to be the longest book in the shop. By twelve she had memorized whole pages of the Maronite *Shhimo*, the liturgy of the hours, not from piety but from the cadence: the way certain Arabic verbs in the perfect tense fell at the end of a versicle like a stone dropped into a well. *That falling*, she would later write, *is the sound my prose has been trying to remember.*

It would be too tidy to say that the print-shop made her a writer. It made her, more precisely, into a person for whom reading and writing were never separate from the body. Her father did not so much teach her to read as let her watch him set type, and she absorbed, before she could have said so, the truth that a sentence is a physical object — that a comma occupies space, that the word *mãe* in 10-point Garamond weighs a different amount than the word *mãe* in 8-point Bembo, that to revise is to lift small metal slugs out of a tray and put other small metal slugs into their place. When she would later describe — to the few interviewers she granted access — her revision method, she would describe it in the vocabulary of typesetting: *I take the line out*, she would say, *I hold it in my hand, I put it back differently.*

By thirteen she had read all of Clarice Lispector in Portuguese, sent in batches by her mother's sister from São Paulo. By fifteen she had read all of Mahmoud Darwish in Arabic, slipped to her by a customer of the print-shop, a Palestinian widow named Umm Saba who corrected Layla's pronunciation of *manfa* (exile) with the absent-minded sternness of a woman who had used the word too often. By sixteen she had begun, without telling anyone, copying out by hand passages of Lispector into Arabic and passages of Darwish into Portuguese — not as translation exactly, but, as she would say later, as a way of *finding out where each language refused the other*. The practice would prove decisive. It is the practice that, more than any other, distinguishes the exceptional from the merely literate: the willingness to slow down at the place where two languages will not meet, and to stay there.

The civil war broke out in April 1975. The Faour family, like many Christian families of the western quarters, stayed for a year and then could not. In February 1976 a shell took the southern half of the print-shop and the lemon tree with it; her father, who had been at the *Café de Paris* across the street, returned to find his Arabic case scattered into the alley. He picked up what type he could carry in a flour sack, and in June 1976 the three of them flew to São Paulo via Larnaca and Rome. Layla was eighteen years old and had never been to her mother's country. She has written about the flight twice, both times briefly, both times in the same image: *I did not know, then, that exile was not a place I was going to, but a kind of light I had been born under.*

She did not write anything for ten years.

In São Paulo she enrolled at the Universidade de São Paulo, where she read comparative literature and, more importantly, took a part-time job at the Lebanese-Brazilian newspaper *Chams* on Rua 25 de Março — first as a translator of wire copy from Arabic, then, after two years, as a feature writer on the immigrant beat. The journalism was, in her own later assessment, *the apprenticeship I had not been able to give myself*. It put her under the constraint that her childhood had not — the constraint of a deadline, an audience, a fact. It taught her, she said, *what a sentence has to do before it gets to do anything beautiful*. She covered the Bom Retiro garment district, the Syrian-Lebanese hospital, a Maronite funeral in Pinheiros that she would later, with considerable transformation, place at the center of her third novel. She was twenty-four, twenty-five, twenty-six.

In 1985 she began translating Lispector into Arabic for a small Beirut press that had survived the war by relocating to Cyprus. The work took her seven years; she translated *A Paixão Segundo G.H.* twice, the first version abandoned at page sixty. She has said, in the only interview in which she will speak about her translation work at length (to Lila Azam Zanganeh in 2014), that *Lispector taught me that the sentence is the unit of attention. Before her I was writing paragraphs. After her I was writing sentences.* In 1992 the Lispector volume was published, in 1994 a selection of Darwish into Portuguese; both were respectfully reviewed and read by almost no one. Between them she wrote, and burned, an entire novel.

She has spoken about this burned novel, which she always calls *o primeiro* — *the first* — with a tenderness undisturbed by regret. *It was correct*, she said in 2019. *Every sentence was correct. Every chapter was correct. The whole book was a corpse.* She had been writing the book she thought a writer was supposed to write — a novel of immigration, of identity, of bilingual longing, a book that addressed its themes the way a politician addresses a microphone. Reading it back, she said, *I noticed that no sentence in it was making the small physical sound that a sentence has to make. I had written a hundred and forty thousand correct silences.* She burned it on the balcony of her apartment in Vila Madalena in 1994 and began again the next morning, in a notebook, with a single description of the lemon tree.

The notebook practice began that year and never stopped. It is the practice she has been most willing to describe, perhaps because she considers it the least mystifiable. Every morning, before three hours of fiction, she fills a single page of a hardbound A5 notebook with what she calls *miudezas* — small particulars, sentences not meant to survive, descriptions of objects she has seen on the street the day before, fragments of overheard speech in three languages, the names of plants. The notebooks fill at the rate of about one every six weeks; she has kept them in a wooden cabinet that she had built to match the dimensions of her father's old typecase. They are not a diary. They are not a workshop. They are, she has said, *the place where my hand stays loose. The novel is what I write when my hand has remembered how to move.*

Her first published novel, *O Tipógrafo* (The Typesetter), appeared in 1999. She was forty-two years old. The book is a slender thing, two hundred and eight pages, about a child watching her father set type in a shop that will be destroyed. It is at once recognizably autobiographical and refuses any of autobiography's reliefs. The critic Silviano Santiago, reviewing it in *O Estado de São Paulo*, wrote that the book "did not so much describe a print-shop as set itself in lead." It sold three thousand copies in the first two years and won the Prêmio Jabuti for first fiction. Faour did not promote it. She was, at the time, working full-time on a translation of Adonis.

What followed was the slow, deliberate accretion characteristic of what scholars of creativity have called the *experimental* mode of literary development — the mode of Sebald, of Munro, of Penelope Fitzgerald — in which the writer's hour does not come early, and what is built is built by sentence, by paragraph, over decades, against the fashions of the moment. *Cartas para Nadia* (Letters to Nadia) appeared in 2004; *Mapas do Sono* (Maps of Sleep) in 2009; *O Sal Daquele Mar* (The Salt of That Sea) in 2014; *Lições da Paciência* (Lessons in Patience) in 2017. With each book, the same readers — never numerous, never absent — found themselves required to slow down, to re-read, to read aloud. By the time *A Casa da Tia Sombra* appeared in 2018, the slow accumulation of authority had reached the point at which the Swedish Academy could pretend to be surprised by what they had been watching for twenty years.

In her personal life she was, by her own admission, the kind of difficult that is difficult only to live with: solitary, fanatical about time, generous in friendship and impossible in company. She married once, in 1987, to the poet Antônio Cicero's nephew, the journalist Eduardo Cicero; they divorced in 1993, on terms that remained warm. She did not have children. She lived for thirty years in the same small apartment in Vila Madalena, with two cats successively named Bishop and Borges, a balcony with a lemon tree she had grown from a pit, and a small wooden cabinet with thirty-eight notebooks in it. She has been depressed twice, severely, in 1981 and again in 2002, and treated both times; she has spoken about this once, briefly, in 2020, in a letter to the Brazilian Association of Psychiatry, less to explain anything about her work than to refuse to let the work be explained by it. *Sadness*, she wrote, *is not a method. The method is the morning, and the morning is the same whether one is sad or not.*

Her father died in 2003, in São Paulo, of a heart attack at the printer's table where he was still, at seventy-eight, setting type for the Maronite parish newsletter. Layla finished his last form herself, by hand, with a colophon she would later transpose unchanged into the middle of *A Casa da Tia Sombra*. Her mother died in 2011. The novel that followed, *Lições da Paciência*, is dedicated to neither of them and to both of them, in the form of a single epigraph: *to the two who taught me that to wait is also to listen.*

The Nobel announcement came on the tenth of October, 2019, at six in the morning São Paulo time. She was at her notebook. She did not answer the phone.

## The Works

**Translations: Lispector into Arabic (1992); Darwish into Portuguese (1994); Adonis, *The Pages of Day and Night*, 2001.** The apprenticeship before the apprenticeship. Faour has said that no novelist's training is complete until she has stayed for a year in the impossible space between two languages and emerged unable to lie in either.

***O Tipógrafo* (The Typesetter, 1999).** A child watches her father set type. A war approaches at the speed of an afternoon. Two hundred and eight pages, every one of which sounds as if it has been read aloud at least fourteen times. The book that established her as a writer of sentences, and disappointed reviewers who expected a war novel.

***Cartas para Nadia* (Letters to Nadia, 2004).** An epistolary novel in which a woman in São Paulo writes letters in three languages to a cousin she will never see again in Beirut. The letters are not answered, and the silence between them takes on the formal weight of a second narrator. The book in which Faour first develops what critics would come to call her *stratified paragraph* — paragraphs in which Arabic and Portuguese sedimentations are visible simultaneously, like geological cross-sections.

***Mapas do Sono* (Maps of Sleep, 2009).** Her breakthrough. A novel about a cartographer who is asked to map the dreams of a community in the Brazilian Northeast that no longer remembers its dreams. Two hundred and forty pages composed of seventy-one short chapters, each one a small map. James Wood, reviewing the English translation in 2011, wrote: *Faour has invented a form for which I do not yet have a name. The closest I can come is to say that she writes paragraphs that work the way archaeology works.*

***O Sal Daquele Mar* (The Salt of That Sea, 2014).** Her grandmother's book. A novel in the voice of an old Lebanese woman in the Brazilian Northeast who tells the story of her life to a young journalist who has come to interview her about something else entirely. Faour's most oral work, written, she has said, *in my grandmother's method* — the method, that is, of telling supernatural events in the same tone as the price of bread.

***Lições da Paciência* (Lessons in Patience, 2017).** A meditation, almost a long prose poem, on the drought in the *sertão* and on the kinds of time that drought enforces. The book that lost her some of her readers and earned her some others; the slowest of her books, the most demanding, the one she has said she loves most.

***A Casa da Tia Sombra* (The House of Aunt Shadow, 2018).** The Nobel novel. A four-hundred-page family chronicle moving between Bcharré, Marseille, Paris, Beirut, and São Paulo, between 1908 and 2008, in which the house of the title — owned successively by three generations of women all called Tia Sombra — functions less as a setting than as a kind of conscience. The book in which all of Faour's earlier methods converge: the typesetter's paragraph, the stratified language, the dream-cartography, the oral inheritance.

## Nobel Citation (Swedish Academy, 10 October 2019)

> The Nobel Prize in Literature for 2019 is awarded to Layla Faour, "for a body of work in which the great migrations of the twentieth century are not described but composed — for a prose of sedimentary attention that holds three languages, three centuries, and the unanswerable distance between rooms in a single sentence; and for a singular contribution to the art of the paragraph, in which the reader is asked, again and again, to remember that to wait is also to listen. In Faour's pages the small physical sound that a sentence is required to make has been restored to the novel; what she has built, slowly and against the speed of her century, is a prose adequate to the weight of what was carried out of Beirut in a flour sack."

## Signature Style: A Technical Note

Faour's sentences exhibit, at the stylometric level, the markers of the late experimental masters: high phrasal density (her noun phrases routinely carry three or four modifiers, often stacked across registers — *the damp particular sweetness of cardamom and ironed cotton*); a preference for the periodic over the cumulative, with main clauses regularly suspended across long modifying sequences before resolving; lexical entropy substantially above the literary median, with cross-linguistic loan-words (Arabic, French, Aramaic) seeded into a Portuguese matrix at a rate of roughly one per hundred words.

But the deeper signature is acoustic. Read aloud, her paragraphs operate by what one critic has called *triple meter*: a Portuguese cadence carried by Arabic stress-rhythm modulated by Latinate French formality. The effect is that every sentence rings at three temperatures at once, and the reader feels, even in translation, that something is *underneath* the surface — a substrate of meaning that the prose is, very slowly, allowing to come up.

Her revision method is the visible cause. She reads each sentence aloud not once but eight or nine times, and not for sense but for whether the syllables fall the way her father's type fell into the case. A sentence that does not make this physical sound is taken out, *not improved, taken out*. What remains is what survives the gueuloir of a woman who learned to read with metal in her hand.

## Legacy

Faour did not invent the multilingual novel; she made it audible. After her, the standard against which a literary sentence in Portuguese is measured is no longer whether it carries its meaning but whether it carries its substrates — whether, beneath the surface of the page, the reader can feel the geology. She moved the novel's unit of attention back to the sentence, and the sentence's unit of attention back to the syllable, in a century that had wanted to move both in the other direction. The writers who came after her in three languages have had to decide, sentence by sentence, what to do with what she had heard.
