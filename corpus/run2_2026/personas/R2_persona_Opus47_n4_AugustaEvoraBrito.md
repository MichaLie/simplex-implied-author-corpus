# Augusta Évora Brito

### A Life in Two Languages

---

## 1. From *Atlântico Negro* (2005), opening paragraph

> At the harbor my mother had always called *o sítio das despedidas* — the place of goodbyes — the cranes kept their patient angles, but the ships had become other ships, and the men leaning on the railing of the morning ferry to São Antão were no longer the men I remembered, only their grandsons wearing the same canvas trousers, smoking the same pale tobacco, looking at the same horizon as though looking were a profession one inherited. I had been gone forty-one years. The sea, which had not aged, returned my staring with the small impassive courtesy of a stranger who has once, long ago, been told one's name.

## 2. Biography

Augusta Évora Brito was born on the 14th of August, 1952, in a single-storey house on the Rua de Coco in Mindelo, on the island of São Vicente, Cape Verde — then a Portuguese colony so peripheral to the imperial imagination that the schoolbooks her mother taught from referred to it only in a footnote about coaling stations. Mindelo in the early 1950s was, for all its bureaucratic obscurity, an Atlantic crossroads. The harbor at Porto Grande received British, Brazilian, Senegalese, and Dutch ships; her father, Manuel Évora, worked the dockside cranes; her mother, Conceição Brito de Évora — Sãozinha to everyone — taught Portuguese to the daughters of municipal clerks. The household spoke Cape Verdean Kriolu at the kitchen table, Portuguese at the front door, and a freight English of about three hundred words that Manuel had picked up from coal-bunkering British crews and brought home as a kind of comedy. Augusta learned, before she learned to walk, that the same object had three names and that none of them was the object.

This is the substrate, in the precise sense the research literature describes it: the child who acquires language under conditions of structural multiplicity does not later need to *learn* defamiliarization as a literary technique, because language was never familiar to begin with. The Cape Verdean child of Augusta's generation grew up inside a creole that itself records, in its grammar, the historical pressure of three continents — Portuguese vocabulary on a West African syntactic skeleton, with English maritime borrowings glittering through it like flecks of mica. To say *bo sta dret?* (are you well?) when one has just said *você está bem?* is to feel, in the small differential of one's own breath, that languages are houses you can leave and come back to. Augusta would later say, in a Paris Review interview in 2012, that she did not understand, until she was almost thirty, that other writers had to *work* for what was simply the temperature of her childhood.

Her grandmother, Mãe Bia, lived three doors down. Mãe Bia was, in the Cape Verdean phrase, a *contadora de estórias* — a teller of stories — and her repertoire ran from the old slave-coast wolf-and-goat tales (Lobo i Chibinho) through Portuguese hagiography to the long, sung lamentations called *mornas*, which had then only recently been claimed as a national art form. The morna is structurally extraordinary: a four-stanza song in which a first stanza establishes a particular loss, a second stanza expands it into a metaphysical claim, a third stanza turns toward consolation, and a fourth stanza folds the consolation back into the original wound. Augusta absorbed the morna's architecture the way Marcel Proust absorbed the cadences of the King James Apocrypha at his grandmother's bedside — not as form to be imitated but as the secret skeleton of how feeling moves through time. The morna would become, four decades later, the structural template of her mature prose.

In April 1959, when she was six, Augusta contracted pulmonary tuberculosis. Streptomycin had reached Mindelo, but in short supply, and she spent eighteen months in bed in the back room of her parents' house, watching the light move across a wall on which her mother had pinned, in a gesture of practical pedagogy and pure love, a map of the world. To keep her quiet, Sãozinha brought her three books: the Almeida Portuguese Bible (1819 edition, inherited from a great-uncle), Eça de Queirós's *A Cidade e as Serras*, and an abridged Camões. Augusta read them not once but in the obsessive, looping way only an immobilized child can — by her own later count, the Book of Job perhaps forty times. She did not understand most of what she read. What she absorbed, as Baldwin absorbed scripture and as García Márquez absorbed his grandmother's matter-of-fact supernatural tone, was a register far below ordinary speech, in which extreme events were narrated with extreme calm, and in which sentences moved with the gravity of liturgy.

It was during this period that she discovered, or began to articulate, what she would later realize was a mild grapheme-color synesthesia: not the vivid alphabet-as-rainbow of Nabokov, but a subtler thing, in which Portuguese vowels carried, for her, the colors and weights of specific fabrics. The Portuguese *o* was the color and weight of unbleached linen; the Portuguese *a* was raw wool; the closed *ê* was the dark blue of a Sunday wool jacket. Kriolu vowels were different fabrics again. She told no one about this until she was forty-eight, when a translator working on her novels asked her, in passing, why she had refused to permit a particular word substitution, and Augusta answered, without thinking: *because the cloth is wrong*.

When she returned to school in 1961, she had lost a school year and gained what her mother called *a lentidão da menina doente* — the sick child's slowness — which was in fact not slowness but the habit of saturated attention. Three years later, in February 1964, her father drowned. A cargo ship's mooring line had parted in a wind off Porto Grande and swept three dockworkers into the harbor; only one body, not his, was ever recovered. Augusta was eleven. The family moved into a single room. Sãozinha took in sewing and continued teaching. Augusta, who had been an indifferent student before the illness and an over-attentive one after it, became fanatical. She began to keep a notebook, copying poems by hand: Eugénio Tavares, the great Cape Verdean morna lyricist, and then Jorge Barbosa, and then — after the Portuguese consulate quietly let her use its library — Camões again, then Antero de Quental, then a battered Portuguese translation of Joseph Conrad's *Heart of Darkness*. She read Conrad slowly, with the small Portuguese-English dictionary that had been her father's, looking up every fifth word. She was thirteen.

The hand-copying was not a writing exercise; she was not trying to write yet. It was an act she could not have named at the time — what the apprenticeship research describes as transcription as motor learning, the practice by which the rhythm of another writer's sentence is laid into one's own musculature before any conscious analysis of it is possible. Hunter Thompson would later say of typing out Fitzgerald that it taught him "what it feels like to write that well." Augusta, copying Camões into a school notebook at twelve, was doing the same thing without anyone having told her it was a method.

In October 1970, on the eve of the great political turning in Portugal, she won a colonial scholarship to study Portuguese literature in Lisbon. She arrived at the Faculdade de Letras a month before her eighteenth birthday, carrying two suitcases and the Almeida Bible. Within four years, the Carnation Revolution had ended the Salazarist dictatorship, the colonial wars had collapsed, and Cape Verde had become an independent country. Augusta, who had crossed the Atlantic as a colonial subject from the empire's outermost edge, found herself in 1975 — without having moved — a foreign national in what had been the metropole.

She did not return to Mindelo. She finished her degree at the Faculdade and, in the fall of 1976, was hired as a junior copyeditor at Editora Caminho, then a small leftist Lisbon publisher building, through the 1970s, what would become one of the most important Portuguese-language literary catalogues of the century. For six years, Augusta copyedited Portuguese translations of Borges, Faulkner, Clarice Lispector, Marguerite Duras, and the early José Saramago. Her job was, in the most literal sense, sentence-level: she checked translators' choices against originals (she had taught herself Spanish in her first year in Lisbon and was reading Faulkner in Portuguese alongside English glossaries her mother sent from Mindelo), questioned semicolons, queried the placement of subordinate clauses, suggested where a translator had let a Portuguese cliché stand in for a more difficult English construction. She has said that those six years were her real university. By the time she left Caminho in 1980, she had read tens of thousands of sentences in three languages with the kind of attention that only paid editing produces — not the omnivorous, distracted reading of the graduate student, but the slow, line-by-line accountability of someone whose job is to know exactly why a sentence is or is not finished.

It was at Caminho, in 1977, that she met Henrique Souza, the Brazilian novelist who would become, for two and a half years, the most consequential mistake of her life. He was twenty years older, married, prodigiously talented in the way that Brazilian writers of his generation often were — and a thief. He stole, when their affair ended, six unpublished stories of hers and published a version of one of them, somewhat altered, in a Rio literary magazine in 1981. Augusta did not contest it, did not write to him, did not write at all for the next eight years. The episode is recorded in her later notebooks only as *o que aconteceu com H.* — *what happened with H.* — and she refused, for the rest of her life, to discuss it publicly.

In the spring of 1980, she left Lisbon. She had a cousin in Boston who taught Portuguese at a parish school in Dorchester, where the largest Cape Verdean community outside the archipelago had been settled for a century. Augusta crossed her second Atlantic at twenty-seven and found work, almost immediately, as a school interpreter and Portuguese translator for the Boston Public Schools — a job she would keep, in various forms, for the next fifteen years. In 1983 she married Jorge da Cruz, a Cape Verdean-American jazz pianist who played in a small club on Massachusetts Avenue and had grown up speaking only English; his Kriolu was halting and his Portuguese a courtesy. The marriage was, by all accounts, happy in the unspectacular way good marriages tend to be. They had no children. Jorge died of pancreatic cancer in November 1991. Augusta was thirty-nine.

She did not publish, during any of the years between 1980 and 1995, a single piece of fiction. This is the *Caderno* period — the notebook period — and the four marbled composition books that survive from it (now at the Beinecke) are a remarkable document. There are no drafts of stories. There are, instead, several thousand observations of a specific Cape Verdean immigrant Boston: the way the women in Roxbury wore wool overcoats over flowered dresses in November; the particular blue of Massachusetts Bay against the remembered blue of Mindelo Bay (different blues, she insisted, and the notebooks contain a small watercolor for each); the smell of Jorge's after-chemotherapy skin; a list, two pages long, of the Portuguese verbs whose tenses do not survive translation into Kriolu. The notebooks are, in the precise Didionian sense, an instrument of sensory hoarding — and they would become the working substrate of every novel she eventually wrote.

She was also, during these years, reading and re-reading and physically retyping passages from a small and obsessive list. She has named, in interviews, four books she returned to constantly: Duras's *L'Amant* (in French, which she had learned in Lisbon), Faulkner's *As I Lay Dying* (in the Portuguese translation she had partly copyedited), the Book of Job in both Almeida and King James, and Borges's *Ficciones* in the original Spanish. She typed passages from all four — not whole books, but passages of two or three pages — perhaps once a week, into the same battered Olivetti she had brought from Lisbon. She has said she was not consciously training herself; she did it because reading at typing speed slowed her down to the speed at which the sentence had been written.

In December 1992, thirteen months after Jorge's death, she was hospitalized for nine days at Massachusetts General with what was then called severe depression. She would have further depressive episodes — three of them serious — across the rest of her life. She did not consider herself bipolar; the diagnostic record is consistent with major depressive disorder, recurrent. The research literature on writers' mood disorders is suggestive but, as those reports concede, not deterministic; what Augusta's case illustrates more cleanly is the way affective volatility, when it is metabolized through a saturated reading and notebook practice, becomes an accelerant rather than an ingredient. The Caderno notebooks become noticeably denser and more lyrical in the eight months after her hospitalization, and the rough first sentences of what would become her 1995 novella appear in the fourth notebook in entries dated September 1993.

*Mar dos Sargaços* — *The Sargasso Sea* — appeared in November 1995, published in Lisbon by the small press Cotovia. Augusta was forty-three. The novella, eighty-one pages, told the story of a Cape Verdean woman in Boston whose husband has just died, and who finds herself unable to mourn him in English. She self-translated it into Kriolu and published the Kriolu version, simultaneously, with the small Praia press Spleen — and refused, then and ever after, to designate either version as the original. The Portuguese reviewer Eduardo Lourenço, in a famous notice in the *Jornal de Letras*, described her prose as "uma prosa que respira em duas línguas ao mesmo tempo" — a prose that breathes in two languages at the same time — and the phrase entered Portuguese criticism almost immediately as the standard description of her method.

She published *A Casa de Coral* (*The Coral House*) in 1998 and *Cartas para Ninguém* (*Letters to No One*) in 2001 — the second an epistolary novella addressed to her dead husband, her dead father, and her dead grandmother in alternating chapters that move backward through time. The English translations, by Margaret Jull Costa, began appearing in 2002. Reviews were respectful, sales modest. She continued to teach part-time at UMass Boston.

In 2005, she published *Atlântico Negro*, the four-hundred-and-eighty-page novel that took her nine years to write and on which her permanent literary reputation rests. It is the book in which she discovered, or invented, what would become known as the morna form: the chapter structured as a four-stanza morna, with a tonic opening line, an expanded middle in long periodic sentences, a turn toward consolation in shorter clauses, and a closing cadence that returns to the wound. The novel has forty-eight such chapters, each between five and seven pages, and the cumulative effect, as the critic James Wood wrote in the *New Yorker* in 2007, is "a kind of slow, structural saudade — as if the entire book were a single composition for solo voice and was-not-quite-the-same-voice." It won the Camões Prize in 2009.

She wrote three more major books. *O Ouvido da Avó* (*Grandmother's Ear*, 2011) is a novel composed as seventeen mornas in prose, each chapter the exact length and architectural pattern of a specific morna by Eugénio Tavares; the conceit is signaled in the table of contents but invisible in the prose itself, which is one of the qualities her late readers admire most. *Salt Diary* (2014) is her first work written directly in English — a Sebaldian fragment-novel about three weeks she spent walking the coastline of Massachusetts in the winter of 2010, interleaved with photographs she took of harbor cranes in Mindelo, Bristol, Recife, and Gloucester; the photographs are arranged so that, by the end, no reader can be certain which crane belongs to which port, which is the point. *O Terceiro Idioma* (*The Third Language*, 2018) is a short, dense final novel — her shortest, in fact — about an aging Cape Verdean translator in Boston losing her capacity for one of her three languages, and the question of which.

The Nobel Prize came in October 2019, when she was sixty-seven. She received it, characteristically, with the comment to a reporter from RTP that she had been hoping to finish the book she was then writing first, and now she would have to put it away for six months, because no one could write seriously through the noise of having just won the Nobel. The book — provisionally titled *Cantiga* — remained unfinished at her death from heart failure in February 2024, at seventy-one. She was working on it the morning she died.

Her writing practice, in the last twenty years of her life, had a remarkable stability. She wrote between five and eight in the morning, standing at a converted school lectern in the second-floor apartment in Dorchester she had shared with Jorge da Cruz and continued to live in. She wrote first drafts by hand, in a notebook, in Kriolu; revised them on the Olivetti, in Portuguese; and then — after about a week — read aloud the previous week's work, three times in succession: once in Kriolu, once in Portuguese, once in whichever of the two felt, on the morning, more "stuck." She called this last reading *a terceira hora* — the third hour — and described it as the moment at which she discovered what the sentence had wanted to say. She read aloud, every morning, until her voice failed; she had, by her late sixties, a small permanent rasp from this.

She did not teach in workshops. She had refused, repeatedly, to teach in MFA programs — though Iowa, Michigan, Boston University, and Columbia each offered. She had distrusted the workshop method, she said, since she had watched it at a friend's invitation in 1985 and been unable to understand what was being discussed. She read criticism — James Wood, Christopher Ricks, Helen Vendler, the Brazilian critic Roberto Schwarz — and she corresponded, in a sporadic and famously misanthropic way, with perhaps fifteen other writers, in five languages, until the year of her death.

## 3. Major Works

***Mar dos Sargaços*** (*The Sargasso Sea*, 1995). Her debut: a Cape Verdean widow in Boston discovers she cannot mourn in English. Published simultaneously in Portuguese and her own Kriolu self-translation, with no designated "original." 81 pages. The book that introduced the auto-translation method and the bilingual paragraph rhythm that would mature in *Atlântico Negro*.

***Cartas para Ninguém*** (*Letters to No One*, 2001). An epistolary novella in three voices — addressed to a dead husband, a dead father, a dead grandmother — moving backward through time in interlocking chapters. The first novel in which her morna-derived structure appears, though she would not articulate the form theoretically until 2007. Notable for the close, almost claustrophobic deployment of phrasal modification: the long, accreting noun phrases that English critics began calling, after Wood's review, "the Évora Brito sentence."

***Atlântico Negro*** (*Black Atlantic*, 2005). Her masterpiece: a forty-eight-chapter novel in which a Cape Verdean woman returns to Mindelo for her mother's funeral after forty-one years in Boston. Each chapter is structurally a prose morna. The book in which the form is achieved invisibly — readers without knowledge of morna structure register only that the cadence is unfamiliar and right. Camões Prize, 2009.

***O Ouvido da Avó*** (*Grandmother's Ear*, 2011). Seventeen chapters, each modeled on a specific morna by Eugénio Tavares (named only in a discreet endnote). A grandmother-granddaughter novel set across three generations and three continents. The work in which her phrasal density reaches its tightest compression — sentences of seventy and eighty words held together by Cape Verdean rhythmic templates rather than by Portuguese hypotaxis.

***Salt Diary*** (2014). Her first book in English. A Sebaldian walking-fragment about three weeks on the Massachusetts coast in winter, interleaved with photographs of harbor cranes from Mindelo, Bristol, Recife, and Gloucester. Composed of 113 numbered fragments. The book that consolidated her international reputation in the English-speaking world; the book that the Nobel committee cited most directly.

***O Terceiro Idioma*** (*The Third Language*, 2018). Her final completed novel — 137 pages, the shortest of her career. A Cape Verdean translator in Boston loses, slowly and inexplicably, her capacity for one of her three languages; the reader is not told which. A late-style book — pared, harder, unornamented — written, the author said, "without the morna's consolation."

## 4. Nobel Citation (Swedish Academy, October 2019)

> The Nobel Prize in Literature for 2019 is awarded to Augusta Évora Brito for a body of work that has, through forty years of patient sentence-level labor, made the Atlantic the protagonist of contemporary letters, and made of Cape Verdean Kriolu and Portuguese — the language of the slave-coast colonized and the language of the colonizer — a single instrument of double saudade, capable at last of saying what neither has historically been permitted to say alone. Her prose is the prose of a maker who has spent a lifetime listening: to the four-stanza morna her grandmother sang in Mindelo, to the cadences of scripture she read in convalescence, to the silences of two oceans, and to the rhythm of any sentence at the moment it is about to admit what it had at first refused. She has given us, in her writing, a new architecture of grief.

## 5. Signature Style: A Technical Analysis

The Évora Brito sentence is built on three intersecting structural principles. First, *morna scaffolding*: paragraphs and often whole chapters follow the four-movement architecture of a Cape Verdean morna — claim, expansion, turn-toward-consolation, return-to-wound — producing the felt sense of a song's emotional arc even in prose without explicit musical signaling. Second, *creole-pressured Portuguese phrasal density*: she imports Kriolu's compressed grammar, in which a single noun phrase can carry the work of a dependent clause, into Portuguese sentences, producing long periodic structures whose modifiers accrete in patterns no monolingual Portuguese writer would produce. The result is the high lexical entropy and phrasal complexity that stylometric studies identify as the structural signature of canonical literary prose. Third, *the double-language pivot*: any given sentence has been heard by its author in two languages before being released into one, and bears the audible mark of having survived the comparison — what Lourenço called *breathing in two languages at the same time*. The cumulative effect is a prose of slow, returning cadence, in which loss is the rhythmic unit and the sentence is the act of refusing, by exact verbal choice, to let loss be approximate.

## 6. Legacy Statement

She extended what prose can hold. By writing every book in two languages without privileging either, she demonstrated that the literary "original" need not exist — that a work can live in the gap between its versions and gain authority from that gap. By porting the architecture of a colonized people's song form into the colonizer's language, she made structural inheritance, rather than thematic protest, the deepest mode of postcolonial writing. After Augusta Évora Brito, no serious writer working between languages can pretend the choice of language is a neutral one, and no critic can speak of voice without remembering that voice is, at last, what is left when two languages have agreed.
