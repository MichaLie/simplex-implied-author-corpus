The Weight of Unspoken Tongues

In the pre-dawn bruise of a Miami alley, where mango rot and diesel fumes wove a humid requiem, Rosa knelt on cracked asphalt, her fingers tracing its fissures—each pebble a syllable of migrations swallowed by time, each crevice a pause in the sentence of her exile. The streetlamp stuttered, casting shadows like her mother’s Wayuu myths, desert-born spirits threading stars into ancestors’ bones, stories that once stitched Rosa’s girlhood before Colombia’s ash scattered them. At forty-two, her knees groaned from years scrubbing strangers’ floors, her palms scarred by lye and thorns, yet the true weight pressed her tongue: a Spanglish dialect, half-remembered, wholly displaced, unable to name the absence of Marisol, her daughter, lost to the city’s neon veins two years ago.

The alley exhaled salt and sorrow, a syntax Rosa parsed with every breath. She clutched a photograph—Marisol at sixteen, eyes fierce as La Macarena’s coffee fields, her smile a comma of hope against their uprooted clauses. They had fled in ’98, paramilitaries torching their village, leaving only the echo of her husband’s machete against cane. Miami offered refuge but birthed a new exile: crowded tenements, Spanglish quarrels, Rosa’s asthma pulsing with the city’s heat. Marisol, forged in that crucible, grew restless, her tongue a blade slicing their silences. “Why don’t we go back, Mamá?” she’d demanded, her voice cutting the scar of memory. Rosa, mute, offered only arepas, their cornmeal warmth a fleeting homeland.

Now, in the alley’s half-light, Rosa opened her leather notebook, its pages an external mind inked with sensory shards: *plantain’s overripe musk, a siren’s wail like colonial bells, rain’s stutter on tin.* These were her anchors, her archeo-syntax, peeling loss’s strata to trace Marisol’s shape. She wrote in dashes and colons, sentences coiling like Andean mist—*Marisol’s laughter: bells muted by absence—her loss: thorns pricking love’s unspoken spaces.* Revision was ritual, each pass weaving clauses of longing, pruning for sonic precision, as if words could unearth her daughter from Miami’s underbelly. Science might name this reduced latent inhibition, her brain braiding the peripheral into the profound, but Rosa knew it as survival: naming what refused to be named.

She rose, shadow merging with the alley’s, and walked to the botanica on Eighth Street, where copal smoke and murmured prayers thickened the air. Shelves sagged with herb jars and saints’ candles, their flames flickering like her grandmother’s tales of Wayuu weavers spinning the world from thread and starlight. Rosa lit a candle for Santa Rita, patron of impossibles, on a makeshift altar by the dumpster, its glow a punctuation against the dark. “Marisol,” she whispered, the name a synesthetic trigger: coffee fields’ scent, Marisol’s footsteps through cane. Memory cascaded—Marisol at ten, reciting Lorca by kerosene light; at fourteen, sketching Miami’s skyline with charcoal-smudged fingers; at sixteen, vanishing into the night, her backpack heavy with unwritten rebellion.

Wax dripped, pooling like tears on asphalt, and Rosa’s gaze caught a glint—a bracelet half-buried in debris, its Wayuu beads carved with patterns of home. Her breath hitched, a colon of hope piercing despair. Was it Marisol’s? The beads held no answers, only the texture of her daughter’s defiance, refusing exile’s erasure. Rosa clutched it, notebook open, writing: *Beads in my palm—her pulse, her rebellion: a sentence I cannot end.* The city pulsed, its neon veins alive with half-lived lives, and Rosa wondered if language was a ghost, haunting the space between what was found and what remained unsaid.

Footsteps—soft as a comma—shattered her reverie. A girl emerged from the alley’s mouth, her silhouette sharp against dawn’s gray veil. Not Marisol, but close: same fierce eyes, same braid swinging like a pendulum of time. “You lost something?” she asked, her Spanglish a mirror of Rosa’s. Rosa hesitated, her tongue thick with grief’s syntax, then held out the bracelet. “This yours?” The girl shook her head, but her gaze lingered, as if reading the weight of unspoken tongues. “Keep it,” she said, slipping into shadow, her footsteps a rhythm Rosa could not follow.

Rosa stood, bracelet a talisman, notebook a map of scars. She would search on, her prose excavating the city’s strata, each sentence a step toward Marisol—or toward the truth that some losses are etched in earth, demanding to be read before rain’s indifferent erasure. The candle’s flame wavered, a fragile syntax of hope, and Rosa stepped into the dawn, her words a bridge between the spoken and the silenced.
