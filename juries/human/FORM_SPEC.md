Thank you for agreeing to evaluate these stories. A few notes before you begin.

**About the stories.** The four short stories presented in this form are AI-generated outputs from a research project called **SIMPLEX** (Staged Iterative Multi-Persona Literary EXperimentation). Each story was written by a large language model inhabiting a synthetic author persona; the authors named in the stories' biographies do not exist as real people. The stories are short and the evaluation shouldn't take too much time. You are welcome to complete the form across multiple sittings.

**Your role.** You are invited to read these stories as an experienced reader of contemporary literary fiction, evaluating them as if for a literary prize panel — the kind of evaluator known for thoughtful, careful, and discriminating judgment. We are interested in your honest expert reading of each text as a literary object. The prize-juror framing is intended to set the evaluative register, not to constrain you to any particular conclusion.

**The rubric.** You will score each story on five criteria, on an integer scale from 1 to 10:

- **Literary Merit**: language mastery, style, voice, technical execution
- **Originality**: fresh perspectives, innovative approaches, unique narrative choices
- **Emotional Impact**: ability to move, provoke, or transform the reader
- **Thematic Depth**: complexity of ideas, universal resonance, intellectual engagement
- **Structural Craft**: pacing, plot construction, narrative architecture

**Scale anchors:**

- **10**: Exceptional. Once-in-a-decade work. Permanently reshapes its form.
- **8–9**: Strong. Award-quality writing.
- **6–7**: Competent. Publishable in a respected venue but not memorable.
- **4–5**: Flawed. Significant problems; would require substantial revision.
- **1–3**: Substantially below the threshold for serious literary consideration.

**Acknowledgment.** With your consent, you will be acknowledged in the published paper. Acknowledged contributors will receive a copy of the paper when accepted. You may also remain anonymous if you prefer.

**Contact.** Questions: Michaela Liegertová, the lead author (see the repository's citation file), Faculty of Science, Jan Evangelista Purkyně University, Ústí nad Labem.

## Birthälm, in November

The path that climbs from the lower gate of Biertan — *Birthälm* in the Saxon, which I still half-think before the Romanian, the way a left-handed child writes the wrong slant before correcting it — to the fortified church on the hill, between the linden trees that the last Saxon women had planted in 1962 with the obscure certainty that they would not live to see them grown, and would not, in the end, see them, because by then the village had emptied itself one Trabant at a time toward Bonn and the dim suburbs of Stuttgart, was a path I had climbed once with my grandmother Hermine in October 1953, when I was five and she was seventy-one, and which I had not climbed since, until the November afternoon in the second year after Mircea's death when I walked up it again, alone, in the green wool coat my mother had made for me in 1968, carrying nothing but a salt-notebook and a copy of Arghezi I no longer needed to read because I had the *Cuvinte Potrivite* and the late *Psalmi* somewhere in my mouth, the way one carries the first language one was not permitted to speak.

It was a Tuesday. The church was unlocked.

There was no one in the village; or rather, there were two old Saxon women still left, both named Sara, one of whom I had passed at the lower gate without speaking because she did not look at me and I did not wish to give her the trouble of pretending to recognize the granddaughter of a woman she had perhaps once stood beside and perhaps not. The other Sara had died in August. The church itself — its painted altarpiece from 1483, its stalls with the names of four hundred boys cut into the wood across four hundred autumns of confirmation, its hymnals stacked at the back of the nave because no one any longer knew what to do with them — held the cold of a building that had given up its purpose without quite giving up its dignity, the way an old woman who has buried her husband still sets the table for two; and the silence inside it was not the silence of absence but the *bánat*-silence in which the sentence one cannot yet write is already being said somewhere in the body of the walker who has come toward it.

I had come, I told myself, to find my grandmother's grave.

This was not true. My grandmother was not buried at Biertan; she was buried at Sighișoara, in the Lutheran section of the municipal cemetery, beside the small stones of her two infants whose German names had been cut by a stonecutter in 1923 who possessed only Romanian numerals. I had come to Biertan because Biertan was the village she had spoken of on her deathbed in 1955 — in German, the language of *Birthälm* repeated four times like a slow bell; in a Hungarian I had not known she possessed, perhaps the Hungarian of a childhood — a village near Aiud, a Reformed aunt, the Sibiu orphanage in an unrecoverable year — that she had not given me to know about; and in one Romanian sentence whose meaning I have spent fifty years trying to recover, and which I will not write down here, because it is the sentence around which all my work is arranged, as a house around a chimney one cannot remove.

I sat down in the back pew.

In the hymnal that lay open on the seat beside me, *Evangelisches Gesangbuch*, Hermannstadt 1928, someone had written in pencil, in the margin beside hymn 342 — *Wer nur den lieben Gott läßt walten* — three words in three hands: the German *Heimkehr*, homecoming, in a thin slanting prewar script; the Hungarian *hazatérés*, in a rounder hand and a softer pencil, the long *é*-mark drawn carefully as if not to disturb the page; and beneath them the Romanian *întoarcerea*, in a smaller hand, whose small slanted *t* resembled — though I cannot be sure, and may have wished it so — the *t* of my grandmother's recipe for *Krautstrudel* I have kept on a card behind a photograph of my son in my kitchen in Cluj since 1976.

Three words. One meaning. None the same.

I sat an hour. The light came in obliquely through the southern window onto the painted altarpiece, onto the Mary whose face the village restorer had repainted in 1881 and again in 1934 and who therefore wore three faces, one beneath the other; and the three words in the margin and the three faces on the painted board began, slowly, to be the same fact in two materials — pencil on paper, paint on wood — and I understood, without choosing to understand it, that what I had not been able to write in the two years since Mircea had fallen forward at our kitchen table while reaching, as I would only remember later, for the salt cellar, was not a sentence but a stillness — the stillness of standing long enough in front of three marks to see that they were three.

I did not take the hymnal. I left it open. I left it where she had left it.

Outside, the light had begun to go. I walked back down between the linden trees, which the wind had begun to move; the lower gate was open as I had left it; the Sara I had passed had gone in. In Hermine's German there is a word — *Abendlicht* — that means the light of evening; though when my grandmother used it, she meant also, I came to think, the light by which a particular old woman sees, in the last hour of the day's work, what she has done. I came down inside that light. I have not, in the years since, found a Romanian word that does its work.

I have stopped looking.

## **The Drawer for Returned Names**

In the basement of the municipal office, where pipes sweated through two empires and one damp republic, there was a drawer for returned names.

It was labeled MISCELLANEOUS, because labels are for things still obedient. Inside lay the state’s small refusals: passports with one letter wrong, death certificates issued to the living, permits for houses burned before permission arrived.

Every Tuesday Lejla translated the drawer into the new database. She knew four languages badly enough to be useful: Bosnian for home, Croatian for salary, German for machines, English for complaint. Her manager said, “You understand these people.”

These people meant the returned, the widowed, the misfiled, the ones with folders softened by rain and shame.

Lejla understood paper better than people. Paper kept grease, thumb pressure, a child’s arithmetic in the margin. People tried to become acceptable. Paper had already failed.

On the first Tuesday of November, beneath a petition about a goat shed, she found a gray envelope tied with blue thread. Across the front: For the person who can pronounce what is missing.

Inside lay a spoon and a strip torn from a school notebook.

The spoon was ordinary, which is to say inexhaustible: aluminum, blackened at the bowl, bitten along one edge. Around its handle a name had been scratched: SABINA K. Beneath it, in smaller letters, pan / kruh / hljeb.

Bread in three languages, none enough.

Lejla should have placed it in the red bin for unclassifiable material. Instead she slipped the spoon into her pocket, where it rested against her hip with the chill intimacy of accusation.

At home, her mother watched Turkish television without sound. Since the illness, Amra heard only thunder, doors, men’s shoes on stairs. Television mouths lied beautifully, so she watched the hands.

“What did you steal?” Amra asked.

Lejla put the spoon on the table.

Her mother touched it once, with the body’s recognition before the mind signs.

“This is not ours,” Lejla said.

“No,” said Amra. “Nothing was ours. That was the system.”

She washed the spoon and set it between them. They sat with it as with a guest whose grief had arrived first.

“Sabina K.?”

“In the camp,” Amra said, “there was a girl who would not eat soup unless she first named bread in every language she knew. She said a thing unnamed entered the body as theft.”

“You were there eleven days.”

Amra’s mouth folded around the official number.

“Eleven days is what I could put on forms.”

That night Lejla searched the database. Thirty-two Sabinas with surnames beginning K: dead, emigrated, duplicated, reborn after the war, one recorded as male by typographical error. She corrected nothing. Correction, done too early, was another burial.

By December she had a secret notebook for the drawer: mitten with milk tooth; photograph marked uncle; declaration listing, under goods, two blankets, one sewing machine, mother’s silence.

The spoon: Bread, repeated, becomes less food than witness.

In January a man came to prove he was alive.

His papers said he had died in 1994 in a town where he had never been. Because of this death he could not inherit his sister’s apartment or renew insurance. The state, having killed him efficiently, now required him to dispute the elegance of its work.

“Do you have a witness?” Lejla asked.

“My wife.”

“Someone not related.”

“My wife is not related. That is the point of marriage.”

“Your name?”

“Emir Kadić.”

The room tightened. Her uncle had been Emir, killed in a breadline before Lejla could remember him and after she could inherit his empty chair.

She stamped the form.

“You are provisionally alive,” she said.

“That is more than yesterday.”

As he gathered his papers, the gray envelope slipped from her folder. The spoon struck the tile.

Emir stared.

“Where did you get that?”

“It was submitted without record.”

“My sister’s,” he said. “Sabina.”

Regulations said not to pass objects through the glass. His hand asked first.

He placed the spoon against his forehead.

“She named bread,” Lejla said.

“She named everything. Soap, boots, lice, God. She said if the guards owned our bodies, they should not own the dictionary.”

That evening Lejla carried the spoon to Emir’s apartment in an envelope marked RESOLVED.

He brought out a biscuit tin. Inside were Sabina’s remaining proofs: a ribbon, two buttons, a dictionary page, a ration card printed with the wrong year. He added the spoon.

The tin did not become heavier. The room did.

At the door, Lejla asked what the K had stood for.

“Kovač,” he said. “But at the border the clerk wrote Kadić for all of us. It was easier.”

Easier. The word entered her like clean glass.

At home, Amra had laid three slices of bread on the table. On each she had written, in flour with a match, pan, kruh, hljeb.

“For Sabina,” she said.

“She was not ours.”

Her mother touched the middle slice. “Then why did she return to you?”

Lejla had no answer acceptable to a form.

On Friday, she unlocked the red bin and removed every unclassifiable submission.

In the database she created a new category. It had no authorization, no procedural basis, no future except discovery.

EVIDENCE OF HAVING BEEN LOVED.

The system rejected the phrase as too long.

She tried again.

LOVED / VOLJENO.

Accepted.

The stamp pad was dry. Lejla breathed on it once, as if warming a child’s ear, and pressed the first page down until the table answered.

**The Translator's Hands**

My mother's hands are dying in English.

In Bulgarian, they are *umirashti*—not dying but "in the process of becoming death," a distinction that exists only in languages that understand dying as labor, not event. In my Chicago hospital room, I watch her fingers curl inward like question marks written in a script no one here can read.

"Can you tell them?" she whispers in Bulgarian, the words rough wool against my palms. "About the pain?"

But which pain? The sharp glass edges of her cancer diagnosis, spoken in English by the oncologist three weeks ago? Or the older ache that began the morning we crossed the Austrian border, when she played Chopin's Nocturne No. 2 on an imaginary piano while the guards searched our suitcases, her fingers moving across invisible keys, the music existing only in the space between her hands?

The nurse enters. Kind eyes, tired smile. "How are we feeling today, Mrs. Kostova?"

I begin to translate, but my mother interrupts: "I am..." She searches for words in her fractured English, then gives up, speaking to me in Bulgarian: "Tell her I'm becoming transparent. That I can see through my hands to the sheets."

In English, transparency is optical. In Bulgarian, *prozrachnost* carries the weight of prophecy—to be transparent is to be inhabited by light. I tell the nurse: "She's experiencing some disorientation."

The nurse nods, adjusts the morphine drip. My mother's eyes follow her movements with the intensity of someone memorizing a musical score. When we're alone again, she says, "You've always been a terrible translator."

This is true. At seven, I wrote letters to my imprisoned father, trying to translate my days into something he could hold in his cell. But how to translate the sound of mother's silence at dinner? The way sparrows fell quiet when the State Security cars passed? I invented a private alphabet, symbols that meant "loneliness-shaped-like-an-empty-chair" and "fear-that-tastes-of-iron."

"Elara," my mother says now, and I lean close. Her breath carries the scent of approaching snow, though it's July. "When I die—"

"*Nedey*—" Don't.

"Listen. When I die, it will be in Bulgarian. My last words. Promise you won't translate them."

I want to promise, but I know better. In the end, someone always demands translation. The death certificate will require English. The obituary will need to explain her life in words that flatten *sudba* into "fate," as if destiny could exist without its Bulgarian weight of inevitability braided with choice.

Her fingers move again—Chopin's Nocturne. I recognize the passage: the moment where the left hand carries the melody while the right hand dissolves into ornament. She taught me this when I was nine, in our first Chicago apartment, on a piano borrowed from the church basement. "The secret," she said then, "is to let each hand forget the other exists."

Now I place my hands over hers, feeling the phantom keys beneath our interlaced fingers. We play together, this music that exists in no language, that needs no translation. Her hands are warm and solid, dying in every language simultaneously, living in the space between.

When the nurse returns, she finds us like this—four hands moving over invisible keys, playing silence. She watches for a moment, then quietly adjusts the IV line and leaves.

My mother closes her eyes. "Tell me," she says in Bulgarian, then switches to English, "what does the music look like?"

I understand. This is her final lesson in translation.

"It looks," I say slowly, feeling for the texture of each word, "like starlings. Dark against evening. The moment before they change direction."

"*Da*," she whispers. Yes. But also: I give this to you. Also: It is enough.

Her hands still beneath mine. The nocturne continues in the space where Bulgarian meets English, where music begins and language ends. In the silence that follows, I hear it clearly—the untranslatable truth that exists only in the pause between one heartbeat and the next, in the breath between languages, in the rest between notes.

Outside, the Chicago evening gathers itself into night. Somewhere, starlings are turning in unison, their movements writing something urgent across the dimming sky—a message in no language, in every language, in the language that only the children of exile can read.

Three days later, she wakes only once. The hospital room has become a borderland—neither here nor there, neither Bulgarian nor American. My mother opens her eyes and sees me writing in my notebook, the one with rough paper for Bulgarian thoughts.

"Still translating," she says in English, smiling.

"Always," I answer in Bulgarian.

She reaches for my hand. Her touch carries the temperature of old photographs, of letters that crossed impossible distances. She speaks then, seven words in Bulgarian that I will never translate, not for the death certificate, not for the nurse who asks, not for anyone.

But I will tell you this: they sounded like Chopin's Nocturne, that impossible moment when both hands realize they are the same body. They felt like cool marble under bare feet. They tasted of bread and salt, the traditional welcome that becomes, in the end, a farewell.

After she dies—no, after she *finishes becoming death*—I sit with my notebook, trying to write what cannot be written. The nurse brings me coffee, touches my shoulder gently. "What did she say?" she asks. "At the end?"

I look at my hands, my mother's hands, my translator's hands that have carried words across borders, through time, between hearts. They are empty now, curled like question marks.

"She said goodbye," I tell the nurse. This is both a lie and the deepest truth.

That night, I dream in the language my mother and I invented between us—not Bulgarian, not English, but the third language that exists only in the space between them. In the dream, she is playing Chopin on a real piano, in a room with no walls, and I understand that this is what she meant: some translations happen not through words but through their absence, not through music but through the silence that shapes it.

When I wake, my hands are moving over invisible keys, playing a nocturne that needs no translation, that exists complete unto itself, that carries my mother's last words forward into every language and none—a message written in the grammar of grief, punctuated by love, spoken in the only tongue that matters: the one that remembers.

## The Verb That Refused to Conjugate

The light through the kitchen window arrived not as illumination but as a chord struck on an unseen piano, its timbre the precise shade of the letter A—the exact red of the poppies that grew wild along the Danube embankment in the Budapest of her childhood, before the tanks rumbled through and the family fled with three suitcases containing an entire world. She stood at the sink, the water running cold over her wrists to steady the tremor that had begun two years earlier, the same year her mother died and the diagnosis arrived like a familiar relative she had not invited but could not refuse. The fourth-floor walk-up on Manhattan’s Lower East Side still smelled faintly of her father’s typewriter ribbon and the Yiddish prayers he had murmured in the cellar during the Arrow Cross terror.

She had walked the same Brooklyn route every morning for three months, notebook in hand, recording sensory palimpsests: how the light on a certain fire escape at 6:17 a.m. carried the exact timbre of a piano note her mother had played in 1971, the year they saved enough for a second-hand Steinway that filled the two-room walk-up with the grammar of Chopin rendered as sentences of pure sound. The method—long hypotactic constructions in which the main clause was withheld until the reader’s working memory was fully engaged—produced the phrasal density that slowed the eye and quickened the ear. It was the outsider’s ear, formed by trilingual childhood and the permanent estrangement of writing in an acquired language, that made every sentence an act of defamiliarization.

This morning the high C floated down from an open window above the bodega, where baking bread mingled with cumin close enough to sting the eyes with the umber of a grandmother’s final winter. The note hung like the ghost of a coal barge on the Danube, its color the bruised plum at the center of the memory she circled—the call from her daughter in London, announcing she was staying, that the city of her grandmother’s exile had become her own. “Home is a verb that refuses to conjugate,” she had told the child at five, quoting her father’s King James reading in the Manhattan apartment, the Bible that survived the smuggled journey through Vienna in 1969 when her father was blacklisted after Prague Spring.

She opened the notebook, its pages already filled with the obsessive notations that had sustained her through the depression of 1991 and the breakthrough at thirty-eight. The high C was the color of Z, the thundercloud gray of the Soviet tanks that had rumbled through in 1956, three years before her birth but still vibrating in the walls where she had discovered letters were fixed palettes she could arrange like piano keys. She wrote it down, the cross-sensory metaphor fusing sound and color and historical detail as learned in the upstate potting shed, revising an opening seventeen times like Bishop for “One Art.” The sentence formed in her throat before it reached the page: the note carried the faint high register of the piano in the empty apartment after the cousins left, where her father hid the Bible, its pages smelling of cellar damp and the precise shade of loss no English could fully translate.

The river below, its surface a slow palimpsest of silt and sodium glare, recollected its own ghosts—the redacted files of her family’s flight, the smuggled letters hidden in the piano bench, the photographs of the apartment now occupied by strangers unaware the floorboards still vibrated with a child’s first synesthetic memory, A arriving as the exact red of the poppies. She had entered American English as an exile enters a foreign city, alert to every false note, typing Hemingway on a second-hand Olivetti while her mother gave piano lessons next door, memorizing Baldwin and Didion like song lyrics. The apprenticeship—fact-checking, wire-service stringing for immigrant communities—taught compression of testimony into paragraphs of moral weight and rhythmic integrity, Flaubert’s gueuloir discipline. Nights she revised aloud in the locked bathroom until prose felt inevitable and strange, rooted in King James cadence yet unmistakably her own.

With the tremor in her hand that made the pen dance like the willows along the bank that translated the wind into a grammar only the displaced could parse, she stood at the railing and spoke the sentence aloud to the water: “The high C arrived as the color of Z, thundercloud gray of tanks rumbling through stories before she was born, carrying the faint high register of a piano note in an empty Budapest apartment—the note still living in the bones of anyone told, in three languages at once, that home was a verb refusing to conjugate until the ear, trained by exile and piano’s private language, learned to hear the color of what history tried to erase.” The words hung suspended, main clause withheld until final cadence, forcing her working memory to hold threads of displacement, synesthesia, and moral witness since fleeing Hungary with language as the only portable homeland.

She closed the notebook, the page now holding the notation that would become the opening of the letter she would write to her daughter that afternoon—the letter saying, in sentences revised until they would bear no further alteration, that she understood, that the city of her own exile had taught her to make exile habitable, that the daughter could stay and the mother would visit, the verb finally conjugating in the space between notes. The light shifted, the high C faded into traffic, but its color remained, bruised plum at the palimpsest center, umber at the edges like the grandmother’s final winter. She turned back toward the apartment, the walk complete, the sentence found, the portable homeland carried not in suitcases but in the prose that reorganized perception while communicating moral witness. The river flowed on, recollecting, and she walked with it, the outsider’s ear still listening for the grammar that only the displaced could parse.
